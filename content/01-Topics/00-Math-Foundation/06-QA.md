# Probability Q&A

## Q: Why can the PDF of an exponential distribution be greater than 1?

**Asked in:** [Meeting 01](../../04-Meetings/1404-04-27.md)  
**Answer:** The PDF is a **density**, not a probability. For an exponential distribution:

$$
f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}, \quad f(0) = \lambda
$$

If $\lambda > 1$, the PDF can exceed 1. But the total probability is the **area under the curve**, which is always equal to 1.

## Q: What does it mean for the exponential distribution to be memoryless?

**Asked in:** [Meeting 01](../../04-Meetings/1404-04-27.md)  
**Answer:** The exponential distribution satisfies:

$$
P(X > s+t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)
$$

This means the future waiting time does not depend on how long you have already waited. The past does not affect the future because of the independence of time intervals.

---

## Q: What does it mean for the geometric distribution to be memoryless?

**Asked in:** [Meeting 01](../../04-Meetings/1404-04-27.md)  
**Answer:** The geometric distribution satisfies:

$$
P(X > s+t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)
$$

The proof uses the tail probability $P(X > k) = (1-p)^k$. Substituting:

$$
\frac{P(X > s+t)}{P(X > s)} = \frac{(1-p)^{s+t}}{(1-p)^s} = (1-p)^t
$$

Thus, past failures do not influence the probability of needing additional trials.

## Q: What is the difference between covariance and correlation?

**Asked in:** [Meeting 01](../../04-Meetings/1404-04-27.md)  
**Answer:**

- **Covariance:** Measures how two variables change together, with units = product of their units. Range: $(-\infty, +\infty)$.  

- **Correlation:** Standardized covariance:

$$
\rho_{X,Y} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}
$$

It is dimensionless, always in $[-1, 1]$, and easier to interpret.

---

## Q: Why is the correlation unitless?

**Asked in:** [Meeting 01](../../04-Meetings/1404-04-27.md)  
**Answer:** Because dividing covariance ($\text{units of } X \times Y$) by $\sigma_X \sigma_Y$ cancels the units:

$$
\frac{\text{(kg} \cdot \text{cm)}}{\text{(kg} \cdot \text{cm)}} = 1 \text{ (unitless)}
$$

---

## Q: Is it valid to compare correlations from different datasets?

**Asked in:** [Meeting 01](../../04-Meetings/1404-04-27.md)  
**Answer:** Yes, because correlation is standardized and unit-free, so it measures the strength of linear relationships consistently. However:

- Only valid for linear relationships.  
- Sample size and context should be considered.

## Q: We often don't have the real theta, so, how is bias calculated?

**Asked in:** [Meeting 02](../../04-Meetings/1404-05-04.md)  
**Answer (ChatGPT):** 
> ✅ **We almost never know the true parameter θ** (like the true population mean or variance),  
> ❌ So **we can’t compute the bias directly** in practice.

### So how is bias calculated or estimated?
> There are **two main settings**:

### 1. **Theoretical Setting (Simulation or Assumed Population)**

If you know the population distribution (say, Normal(μ=10, σ=2)), or you're simulating data, then you know the true θ.

In this case, you **can** calculate the bias:

$$
Bias(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta
$$

How?

- Generate **many samples** (e.g., 10,000).
    
- Compute the estimate (e.g., sample mean, sample variance) on each one.
    
- Take the **average of those estimates** → that’s E[θ^]\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}].
    
- Subtract the true θ.
    

✅ This is **empirical estimation of bias**.

---

### 2. **Real-World Setting (θ is Unknown)**

In real data problems:
- We **don't know θ** (the population mean, variance, etc.).
- So we **can’t compute true bias directly**.

But we can still:

#### ✔️ Rely on theoretical properties:
- Use estimators that are **proven to be unbiased** (like the sample mean).
- Use the bias-corrected formulas (like $\frac{1}{n-1}$ in sample variance).

#### ✔️ Use Bootstrap or Simulation to Estimate Bias:

> Bootstrap allows you to **approximate the bias** without knowing θ.

How it works:

- Take your one sample.
- Resample (with replacement) many bootstrap samples.
- Calculate your estimator on each one.
- Compute average of those estimates.
    
- Then estimate:
    
    $$
    \widehat{\text{Bias}} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^B \hat{\theta}^{(b)} - \hat{\theta}_{\text{original}}
    $$
    where $\hat{\theta}^{(b)}$ is from bootstrap sample bb, and $\hat{\theta}_{\text{original}}$ is your original sample’s estimate.
    

This gives you an **approximate bias**, even if θ is unknown.

---
### Bottom Line

| Case                                  | Can you calculate Bias? | How?                                   |
| ------------------------------------- | ----------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| You know θ (simulated or theoretical) | ✅ Yes                   | Average over many samples              |
| Real-world data                       | ❌ Not directly          | Use bootstrap or theoretical reasoning |



## Q: Comparing `X` and `X̄` as Estimators of the Mean 
Answer: 
### Goal

Estimate the **population mean** $(\mu)$, based on random samples $( X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n \sim \text{i.i.d.})$ from a distribution with mean $(\mu)$ and variance $( \sigma^2 )$.

We compare two estimators:
- $(X)$: A single random sample (e.g. $(X_1)$)
- $(\bar{X})$: The sample mean:
  $[\bar{X} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i]$

---

### Bias

$[\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \mu]$

| Estimator   | Bias Calculation              | Result     |
| ----------- | ----------------------------- | ---------- |
| (X)         | $(\mathbb{E}[X] = \mu)$       | ✅ Unbiased |
| $(\bar{X})$ | $(\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}] = \mu)$ | ✅ Unbiased |

✅ Both \( X \) and $(\bar{X})$ are unbiased estimators of $(\mu)$.

---

### Variance

- $( \text{Var}(X) = \sigma^2 )$
- $( \text{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} )$

| Estimator   | Variance                |
| ----------- | ----------------------- |
| \( X \)     | $(\sigma^2)$            |
| $(\bar{X})$ | $( \frac{\sigma^2}{n})$ |

✅ $(\bar{X})$ has **lower variance**, and becomes more stable as $( n \to \infty )$.

---

### Efficiency

An estimator is more **efficient** if it has **lower variance**, assuming the same bias.

- $(\bar{X})$ is more efficient than \( X \)

---

### Final Comparison

| Estimator   | Bias | Variance                 | Efficient?       | Recommended? |
| ----------- | ---- | ------------------------ | ---------------- | ------------ |
| \( X \)     | 0    | $(\sigma^2)$             | ❌ Less efficient | ❌ No         |
| $(\bar{X})$ | 0    | $( \frac{\sigma^2}{n})$ | ✅ Yes            | ✅ Yes        |

---

### 💡 Conclusion

- Both estimators are unbiased
- But $(\bar{X})$ is far more **reliable** due to lower variance
- ➤ **Use $(\bar{X})$** when you have more than one sample

## Q: **Why is $( \mathbb{E}[X] = \mu)$** if (X) is just a **single observation**?  

### The Key Distinction: **Random Variable** vs **Realized Value**
When we write:
$[X \sim \text{some distribution with mean } \mu]$
- (X) is not yet a number.
- It’s a **random variable**, meaning it hasn’t "rolled the dice" yet.
- It represents **the process** of drawing one data point at random from a distribution.

## Q: What is the **Normality Assumption** in Linear Regression Analysis? 
Answer: [Read this ](https://medium.com/@christerthrane/the-normality-assumption-in-linear-regression-analysis-and-why-you-most-often-can-dispense-with-5cedbedb1cf4)
- It states that the **residuals** (i.e., the errors: the differences between the observed values and the predicted values) should be **normally distributed**.
- Formally:  
    If your regression model is:
    $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon$
    then we assume that
    $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$

---
### ✅ Why Does This Matter?
- **For inference**, such as:
    - Calculating **confidence intervals**
    - Conducting **t-tests** or **F-tests** for coefficients
- These statistical tests rely on the assumption that residuals are normal, especially when the **sample size is small**.
### ⚠️ What If Residuals Are Not Normal?
- **Large samples**: Central Limit Theorem often makes this less of a problem.
- **Fixes**:
    - Transform the **dependent variable** (e.g., log, square root)
    - Use **robust regression** or **bootstrapping**
    - Try a **non-linear model** if linearity is the issue
        
✅ ** Summary:**

> We assume, without proof, that the **residuals** (errors) are **normally distributed**, so that we can perform certain statistical calculations like p-values and confidence intervals.
> 
> This assumption becomes **less critical when the sample size is large**, because the **Central Limit Theorem (CLT)** makes the sampling distribution of the estimates approximately normal anyway.
> 
> **But if the assumption is wrong and the sample is small**, then:
> 
> - Our statistical inferences (like hypothesis tests and confidence intervals) may become **unreliable or misleading**.
>     
> - We might get **wrong conclusions**, like thinking a variable is significant when it's not (or vice versa).
>     


## Q: Why use Gradient Descent in Linear Regression? Why not just calculate directly?
### When You Don't Need Gradient Descent (in Simple Linear Regression)
For **ordinary least squares (OLS)** linear regression, the loss function is convex and differentiable. We can find an exact solution analytically by setting the derivative to zero:
### Closed-form solution (Normal Equation):
    β̂ = (Xᵀ X)⁻¹ Xᵀ y
✅ Pros:
- Exact
- Fast for small/moderate datasets

---
### When You DO Need Gradient Descent
1. **High-dimensional data (large n or p)**:
   - Computing (Xᵀ X)⁻¹ is expensive: O(n³)
   - Gradient descent scales better
2. **No closed-form available**:
   - Logistic regression
   - Regularized models (Ridge, Lasso)
   - Non-linear models
3. **Deep Learning**:
   - No closed-form solution
   - Must use iterative methods like gradient descent
### Summary Table

| Case                         | Use Closed-Form? | Use Gradient Descent? |
|------------------------------|------------------|------------------------|
| Small linear regression      | ✅ Yes           | ❌ Not needed          |
| Large linear regression      | ❌ Too slow      | ✅ Yes                 |
| Logistic regression / NN     | ❌ No closed-form| ✅ Yes                 |
| Regularized models (Lasso)   | ❌               | ✅ Yes                 |
